TP-14001-01Applies to：ISO 14001:2026 Transition PackRev 2026.1 · prepared 2026-10

01What actually changed (ISO-confirmed)

  • 4.1/4.2 Context: broader environmental conditions — Broader environmental conditions, written into the mandatory text: climate change, biodiversity or ecosystem health, pollution levels and the availability of natural resources now sit in a compulsory paragraph of 4.1 — climate change is no longer an example inside a note but a must-answer item needing a call per dimension; the note under 4.2 adds that interested parties may raise needs around pollution, natural resources, climate change and biodiversity, and a non-legal need becomes a compliance obligation once the organization decides to adopt it. Records to keep: the context analysis updated dimension by dimension, each with a signed relevant/not-relevant call; the interested-party list re-issued wherever new needs appear. How auditors check: they open the 4.1 analysis and verify every dimension has a conclusion traceable to 6.1 risks and opportunities — a blank cell or a stock phrase draws a finding.
  • 6.3 (new): change management — A formally new change-management clause (official title “Planning of changes”): once the organization determines a need for changes that affect or can affect the EMS, the changes shall be carried out in a planned manner and managed; the need can come from internal or external sources (see A.6.3), which lists eight change sources a)-h) — new or modified products, services, processes, equipment and facilities; changes in compliance obligations; changes in knowledge and information; technological developments; changes in material or input specifications; asset changes from mergers, joint ventures or divestments; changes in personnel or external providers (incl. contractors); and business interruptions (supply-chain issues, labour disputes, natural disasters, regime change or war). On the ground: build an environmental change-trigger list so every new project, product, regulation, supplier or facility modification passes an environmental review, with the consequences of unintended changes reviewed. Records to keep: the full chain of review, approval, implementation and verification per change. How auditors check: they sample one real change and walk the chain end to end — back-filled records (same-date signatures, jumping version numbers) are easy to spot.
  • Annex A: three official clarifications — ISO 14001:2026 also carries an informative Annex A, and both ISO and the accreditation side confirm it clarifies three themes: the linkage between organizational context, risks and opportunities and other clauses; the life-cycle perspective (LCP); and alignment with strategy, business processes and the integrated management system approach — Global ACI’s transition document lists them separately as key-concept clarifications. On the ground: these three are the outline for awareness training and internal-auditor retraining; whatever Annex A says is the lens through which auditors will read your system. Records to keep: sign-in sheets and quiz papers for Annex A training across staff and auditors, plus a translation page mapping A.4/A.6/A.8 points into your organization’s own vocabulary. How auditors check: they ask questions in Annex A’s wording — A.4.1 on how environmental conditions interrelate, A.6.3 on change sources, A.8.2 on the a)-j) emergency checklist — and your documents and training materials must answer in the same language.
  • Emergency preparedness: identification in the text, graded response, wider training — Three line-by-line changes in the standard text: ① 6.1.2 now states “the organization shall determine potential emergency situations (see 8.2)” — identification is a hard, explicitly linked requirement; ② 8.2 c) requires actions commensurate with the scale of the emergency and its potential environmental impact — grade scenarios and match response capacity; ③ 8.2 f) extends information and training to interested parties, including persons working under its control — contractors are no longer out of scope. On the ground: document the scenario list as a 6.1.2 output, configure proportionate responses per grade, and include contractors in training sign-ins; BSI paraphrases the identification basis from “reasonably foreseeable” to “all potential”, so the safe move is to include low-probability/high-impact scenarios in your list. Records to keep: the 6.1.2 scenario list, the graded response-configuration table and contractor-inclusive training sign-ins. How auditors check: they walk your plan against Annex A.8.2’s official a)-j) list and ask how each scenario traces to the 6.1.2 evaluation records.
  • 6.1 restructured: planning action split into 6.1.5 — In 2015, risks/opportunities and planned actions shared 6.1.4; 2026 splits them into 6.1.4 “Risks and opportunities” and 6.1.5 “Planning action”, and 6.1.1 requires establishing, implementing and maintaining the processes needed to meet 6.1.2-6.1.5, available as documented information to the extent necessary. On the ground: merge the aspects evaluation and the risk/opportunity register into one structured sheet, and make sure the 6.1 processes themselves are documented — “we did it” is not “we have a process”. Records to keep: a definition document for the 6.1 processes (purpose, inputs and outputs, responsibilities, documented extent) and the unified aspects/risks register. How auditors check: they simply ask “where are the 6.1.2-6.1.5 processes defined?” and compare the definition with actual practice — having one without the other draws a finding either way.
  • “Outsourced” → “externally provided” — Wording shifts to “control of externally provided processes, products or services”, with control or influence limited to processes relevant to the EMS’s intended outcomes and accountability extending beyond organizational boundaries. On the ground: re-review your supplier list — every externally provided process whose environmental performance you influence (transport, waste disposal, chemical suppliers, contractors included) comes under control and evaluation, and name contractors explicitly when communicating environmental requirements to external providers. Records to keep: the updated supplier list, environmental evaluation and monitoring records per external process, and communication evidence showing environmental requirements sent to providers. How auditors check: they sample evaluation and monitoring records for transport / waste / chemical suppliers and ask which external processes you judged “relevant to the intended outcomes” — and on what criteria.
  • fulfil → meet: tighter compliance wording — The verb for compliance obligations tightens from “fulfilment” to “meeting”, and behind the wording sits a stricter evaluation basis: obligations mapped to the legal text verbatim, every call evidenced. It pairs with 9.1.2 evaluation of compliance: establish the evaluation process, set the frequency, act when needed after each evaluation, and maintain knowledge and understanding of compliance status. On the ground: re-issue the compliance register — every obligation carrying its legal-text source, evaluations graded “met / not met” with the evidence for the call. Records to keep: the re-issued register, the latest compliance-evaluation round, and records of compliance-status updates. How auditors check: they pick two or three obligations and ask you to justify the “met” grading on the spot — no justification means the evaluation process is failing.
  • Leadership: stronger accountability — Top-management commitment is itemized a)-i): accountability for the EMS’s effectiveness; ensuring the policy and objectives are established and compatible with strategy and the environment; integrating EMS requirements into business processes; providing resources; communicating the importance; ensuring intended outcomes are achieved; directing and supporting relevant roles; promoting continual improvement; and supporting other relevant roles to contribute their leadership — with A.5.1 grounding it in culture: promoting a culture that engages persons working for the organization, or on its behalf. Within 5.2, the protection-of-environment pledge can be made specific: conservation of natural resources, sustainable resource use, climate-change mitigation and adaptation, biodiversity protection. On the ground: environmental objectives become a standing management-review item, with recognizable top-management decisions in the minutes. Records to keep: review minutes with decisions, policy briefing and sign-off records. How auditors check: they interview top management in person about how environmental objectives connect to strategy — a delegate speaking for them does not count.
  • 9 Internal audit: documented programme & objectives — More rigorous: 9.2.2 requires an audit programme (frequency, methods, responsibilities, planning requirements and reporting) built considering the environmental importance of processes, changes affecting the organization and results of previous audits; the new hard line is 9.2.2 a) — each internal audit shall define the audit objective(s), audit criteria and scope for every single audit. On the ground: write the annual programme as a controlled document and add a mandatory “objective / criteria / scope” column to the plan. Records to keep: the controlled programme, the objective-criteria-scope statement per audit, and the post-audit effectiveness evaluation of the programme itself. How auditors check: they sample the latest internal audit — first whether an objective exists, then whether the report is structured around it, and finally how the programme’s own effectiveness was reviewed.
  • Management review: three-part structure — A more structured review: 9.3 now splits into 9.3.1 general (continuing suitability, adequacy and effectiveness), 9.3.2 inputs and 9.3.3 results. Inputs are itemized: status of actions from previous reviews; changes in external and internal issues, in the needs and expectations of interested parties (incl. compliance obligations), in significant environmental aspects and in risks and opportunities; the extent to which environmental objectives are achieved; information on environmental performance incl. trends (nonconformities and corrective actions, monitoring and measurement results, fulfilment of compliance obligations, audit results); adequacy of resources; relevant communications from interested parties incl. complaints; and opportunities for continual improvement. Results must include any implications for strategic direction and opportunities to improve integration with other business processes. On the ground: re-cut the agenda to the new structure and match inputs and outputs item by item. Records to keep: agenda and minutes organized by sub-clause, plus a decision-tracking log. How auditors check: they read the minutes against the 9.3.2 input list — one missing line is one finding.
  • Clause 10 renumbered: replace references site-wide — Old 10.1 (general) is deleted, continual improvement moves up from old 10.3 to 10.1 — determining opportunities for improvement (see clause 9 and 10.2) and implementing the actions needed to achieve the EMS’s intended outcomes and enhance environmental performance — and nonconformity/corrective action follows as 10.2, with corrective action commensurate with the significance of the effects, including environmental ones. On the ground: replace clause references across controlled documents — old 10.1 citations deleted, old 10.3 citations become 10.1 — and map improvement mechanisms onto 10.1/10.2 inputs. Records to keep: revision history and a completed-reference-replacement checklist. How auditors check: they open any procedure at random and read the clause references — old numbering still there means the revision only changed the cover page.
  • Certification calendar (incl. accreditation milestones) — The official Global ACI-TECH-3-TR 2029-04-30 (M) timeline: published April 2026; accreditation bodies ready by 2026-10-31; certification bodies submit transition declarations by 2027-01-31; AB transition decisions due 2027-04-30 — only then may a CB issue 2026-edition certificates; from 2027-10-31 new initial certificates are 2026-only; 2029-04-30 is the cutoff, binding all ~700,000 accredited 14001 certificates — the earliest of the three systems. On the ground: the 14001 AB/CB milestones are the earliest too, so scheduling this transition audit into the nearest surveillance visit is the first domino of your whole integrated plan. Records to keep: written communication with your certification body confirming it has passed its AB transition decision, plus the transition-audit schedule and plan. How auditors check: at least one witnessed assessment of your CB must be an audit to the 2026 edition before the cutoff — your transition visit may well be that witnessed audit, so nobody will coast through it.

01Clause-by-clause reading

GeneralEditorial changes & terminology

Reference updates, restructuring, renumbering and clarifications. Terminology: “results” is the term for the output of a process (e.g. management review) or the intended result of an objective; “outcome” remains the term for the whole EMS; compliance obligations shift from “fulfilment” to “meeting”; “outsourced” becomes “externally provided”. Evidence auditors look for: a completed terminology calibration across controlled documents — mixed old/new wording means the revision never ran a site-wide review. Common traps: updating the manual but not the procedures and forms; or treating Annex A explanations as auditable requirements — Annex A is informative, but it decides how auditors read your system.

Clause 4Context: life-cycle perspective in scope

The mandatory paragraph of 4.1 pulls environmental conditions into the context analysis — climate change, biodiversity or ecosystem health, pollution levels and natural-resource availability assessed item by item and interrelated (A.4.1); needs of interested parties under 4.2 may land on the same topics; 4.3 requires considering your authority and ability to exercise control and influence over the life cycle of activities, products and services — the life-cycle perspective now extends from aspects and operational controls into scope-setting itself; and 4.4 requires using the knowledge gained in 4.1/4.2 when building the EMS. Evidence auditors look for: item-by-item signed conclusions in the 4.1 analysis, the life-cycle argument in the scope statement, and the scope documented and available to interested parties. Common traps: a scope that names only “this site” and excludes upstream/downstream impacts; or a one-word “relevant” on climate without saying where it bites.

Clause 5Leadership: support all relevant roles

5.1 itemizes top-management commitments a)-i), and points like “ensuring the policy and objectives are compatible with the strategic direction and the environment”, “ensuring intended outcomes are achieved” and “supporting other relevant roles to contribute their leadership” can each be probed; 5.2 carries three policy commitments — protection of the environment (incl. pollution prevention and other organization-specific pledges), meeting compliance obligations, and continual improvement — documented, communicated internally and available to interested parties; 5.3 requires assigning and communicating roles, with b) naming someone who reports EMS performance (incl. environmental performance) to top management. Evidence auditors look for: policy sign-offs, decision traces in review minutes, a roles-and-authorities matrix. Common traps: “climate change” in the policy with nothing corresponding in objectives or reviews; or “support other roles” read as management-only — A.5.1’s culture wording covers everyone working for or on behalf of the organization.

6.1.1General: planning becomes process-based

Establish, implement and maintain the processes needed to meet 6.1.2-6.1.5, documented to the extent necessary. This is the new “process-ize” requirement of 2026: planning is no longer the single action “run an evaluation each year” but a defined process — inputs, outputs, responsibilities, frequency, method. Evidence auditors look for: consistency between the definition of the 6.1 processes and how they actually run. Common traps: reading 6.1.1 as “having files is enough” — auditors ask where the process is defined, not where the form lives; and a register without a process definition is a gap in the other direction.

6.1.2Environmental aspects: life cycle + hard emergency link

Determine environmental aspects and associated impacts you can control or influence within the defined scope, considering a life-cycle perspective (stages incl. raw-material acquisition, design, production, transportation, use, end-of-life treatment and final disposal); when determining aspects, consider normal and abnormal conditions, change (planned or new developments, new or modified activities/products/services, see 6.3) and potential emergency situations — this clause states “the organization shall determine potential emergency situations (see 8.2)”, a hard link to 8.2; apply established criteria for significant aspects, document aspects, criteria and significant aspects, and communicate them across levels and functions. Evidence auditors look for: life-cycle coverage in the aspects table, emergency scenarios as a documented output, communication records for significant aspects. Common traps: the scenario list living only inside the emergency plan and being unreadable from the 6.1.2 evaluation records — the mismatch is the most typical finding.

6.1.3Compliance obligations: determine, access, factor in

Determine and have access to the compliance obligations related to your environmental aspects, determine how they apply to the organization, and take them into account when establishing, implementing, maintaining and continually improving the EMS; compliance obligations shall be available as documented information. Evidence auditors look for: the register mapped to legal text verbatim, accessibility (an intake for legal updates), and the connection to the 9.1.2 evaluation process and frequency. Common traps: a register of obligation names without legal clause sources; or “taking into account” with no trace — obligations change and nothing downstream (aspects, objectives, operational controls) moves with them.

6.1.4Risks and opportunities: external conditions included

When planning the EMS, consider 4.1, 4.2 and the 4.3 scope; determine risks and opportunities related to significant environmental aspects, compliance obligations and other issues or requirements identified in 4.1/4.2 — with purposes incl. assuring the EMS achieves its intended outcomes, preventing or reducing undesired effects (including the potential for external environmental conditions to affect the organization) and achieving continual improvement; risks and opportunities to be addressed shall be documented. BSI’s reading adds: emergency situations also appear under risks and opportunities, not only under environmental aspects. Evidence auditors look for: the risk/opportunity register linked to the 4.1 expansion, significant aspects and compliance obligations. Common traps: a risk register holding only environmental accidents — nobody owns external environmental conditions such as extreme weather hitting suppliers or operations, exactly the direction 6.1.4 names.

6.1.5Planning action: actions need a home

Plan a) actions for significant environmental aspects, compliance obligations, and risks and opportunities; and b) how to integrate those actions into EMS processes (6.2, clause 7, clause 8, 9.1) or other business processes, and evaluate their effectiveness (9.1) — considering technological options and financial, operational and business requirements. Evidence auditors look for: a mapping from each action to its home (objective, operational control or monitored indicator) and effectiveness-evaluation records. Common traps: actions parked in the register with no destination — integration is the hard requirement of 6.1.5, and a floating action means failed planning; the same goes for an action list whose effectiveness has never once been evaluated.

6.2Environmental objectives: measurable, monitored, communicated

6.2.1 establishes environmental objectives at relevant functions and levels, considering significant aspects, related compliance obligations and risks and opportunities; objectives are consistent with the policy, measurable where practicable, monitored, communicated, updated as appropriate and documented. 6.2.2 plans down to “what will be done, what resources, who completes it, when, how results including indicators for monitoring progress will be evaluated”, integrating the actions into business processes. Evidence auditors look for: measurable objectives with monitoring data, evaluation indicators for completion, update records. Common traps: objectives phrased as unmeasurable “comply with regulations”; or objectives nobody has heard of — “communicated” is part of the requirement, not a virtue.

6.3Planning of changes: the environmental gate for change

Official title “Planning of changes”: once a need for changes that affect or can affect the EMS is determined, changes are carried out in a planned manner and managed; the need can be internal or external (A.6.3 lists eight source types a)-h)), and change-management requirements are also scattered across 6.1.2, 7.4.2, 7.5.3, 8.1, 9.2.2 and 10.2. Evidence auditors look for: a change-trigger list, the review-approval-implementation-verification chain per change, and records of reviewing the consequences of unintended changes. Common traps: a change process with an engineering track but no environmental dimension; or a process that no real change has completed end to end in six months — auditors will smell a paper process and ask for the log.

Clause 7Support (minimal impact)

7.1 Resources is a one-line clause: determine and provide what is needed; 7.2 Competence covers persons whose work affects environmental performance and the ability to carry out compliance obligations, with documented evidence; 7.3 Awareness requires knowing the policy, the significant aspects related to one’s work and their actual or potential impacts, one’s contribution to EMS effectiveness, and the consequences of nonconformity (incl. not meeting compliance obligations); 7.4 Communication must consider compliance obligations and keep messages consistent and reliable, with internal communication including changes to the EMS; 7.5 is the usual documented-information triplet. Evidence auditors look for: competence evaluations and training records, ad-hoc awareness questioning by role, documented communication evidence. Common traps: awareness training about the policy only, never about “the significant aspects related to my work” — check item by item against the 7.3 list and the gap shows itself.

Clause 8Operation: external providers + three emergency refinements

8.1 establishes operational criteria and controls to implement the actions from clause 6; controls planned changes and reviews the consequences of unintended changes; applies control or influence over externally provided processes, products and services; and lands four life-cycle items — environmental requirements in design and development at each stage, environmental requirements in purchasing, communicating them to external providers (incl. contractors), and considering the need to provide information on transport, use and end-of-life stages. 8.2 builds the process to prepare for and respond to the potential emergency situations determined in 6.1.2: planned actions to prevent or mitigate (a), responding to actual emergencies (b), actions commensurate with scale and potential environmental impact (c), periodic testing where practicable (d), review and revision particularly after an emergency or test (e), and information and training for interested parties incl. persons working under its control (f) — documented, with Annex A.8.2’s a)-j) consideration list alongside. Evidence auditors look for: operational criteria mapped to clause-6 actions, and the full 8.2 record chain. Common traps: ungraded response capacity, drills without reviews, contractors missing from training rosters.

Clause 9Performance evaluation: objectives for every audit

9.1.1 requires evaluating environmental performance and EMS effectiveness, determining what and how to monitor, measure, analyse and evaluate, the criteria and appropriate indicators for environmental performance, and timing, using calibrated equipment, with performance information communicated per the communication process and compliance obligations; 9.1.2 establishes the compliance-evaluation process and frequency while maintaining knowledge of compliance status; 9.2.2 a) defines objectives, criteria and scope per audit; 9.3 splits into three sub-clauses with inputs and outputs matched item by item. Evidence auditors look for: documented criteria and indicators, calibration records, an objective statement per audit, and the review input/output map. Common traps: piles of monitoring data with no defined “criteria and indicators for evaluating environmental performance” — the chain from data to conclusion is broken; and audit reports not structured around their stated objective, which makes the objective decorative.

Clause 10Improvement: clause 10 renumbered

10.1 Continual improvement: determine opportunities for improvement (see clause 9 and 10.2) and implement the necessary actions to continually improve the suitability, adequacy and effectiveness of the EMS and enhance environmental performance; 10.2 Nonconformity and corrective action: control and correct, deal with the consequences (incl. mitigating adverse environmental impacts), review and determine causes, check whether similar nonconformities exist or could occur, implement actions, review effectiveness, change the EMS if necessary — corrective action commensurate with the significance of the effects, records kept. Evidence auditors look for: tracking from improvement opportunity to action, and the proportionality argument behind each corrective action. Common traps: references still on old numbering (old 10.1/10.3), or corrective actions treated uniformly regardless of significance — proportionality is the auditor’s new lever, and a light touch on a significant environmental impact invites questions.

What the auditor will probe at the transition auditThe eight questions most likely on transition-audit day, each prepared as “question → why it is asked → a good answer → what evidence to show” — environmental auditors are even more evidence-strict than quality auditors.▸

01What the auditor will probe at the transition audit

  • Question: does 4.1 genuinely cover climate and biodiversity, or only in vague terms? Why it is asked: climate change now sits in the mandatory 4.1 text, and the auditor separates “labelled” from “assessed”. A good answer: walk the dimensions with a conclusion and a reason each — for example “for our site climate means extreme rainfall overloading the chemical-store drainage; it is in the risk register and as a plan scenario”. Evidence to show: the 4.1 analysis form (itemized conclusions + signatures) and the trace from each conclusion into 6.1 risks and opportunities.
  • Question: is 6.3 change management a real process, or back-filled? Why it is asked: a brand-new clause is the easiest to paper over, so the auditor validates it on a real change. A good answer: pick one change on the spot (new supplier / process / regulation) and narrate trigger → review → approval → implementation → verification, pointing out where the unintended-change consequence review happened. Evidence to show: the full-chain records of that change, the trigger list, and the last-6-months change log.
  • Question: what scenarios does the emergency plan cover, and does it match the 6.1.2 evaluation records? Why it is asked: 6.1.2 and 8.2 are hard-linked, and a mismatch between the two is the most typical 2026 finding. A good answer: state that the scenario list is a documented 6.1.2 output covering low-probability/high-impact cases, that response grades are commensurate with scenario scale (8.2 c)), and that training includes contractors (8.2 f)). Evidence to show: the 6.1.2 evaluation records, the scenario library, the Annex A.8.2 a)-j) self-check page, contractor-inclusive training sign-ins, and the latest post-drill review.
  • Question: are “externally provided” processes actually controlled, with evidence? Why it is asked: once the terminology changed, the auditor checks whether the control scope moved with it. A good answer: name which external processes were judged “relevant to the EMS’s intended outcomes” and on what criteria, and how evaluation and monitoring reach beyond the boundary. Evidence to show: the supplier list, evaluation and monitoring records for transport / waste / chemical providers, and records of communicating environmental requirements to providers (incl. contractors).
  • Question: how do you judge obligations “met”? Why it is asked: the shift from fulfil to meet tightens the basis, and an unevidenced call means the evaluation process is failing. A good answer: show the verbatim mapping to legal text, the grading method and frequency, and the mechanism that keeps compliance-status knowledge current. Evidence to show: the re-issued register, the latest evaluation round, and the 9.1.2 process document.
  • Question: does management review produce verifiable outcomes under the new three-part structure? Why it is asked: with 9.3 split into three sub-clauses, a missing input and an untraceable output are both findings. A good answer: walk the 9.3.2 input list line by line and show how decisions track to closure. Evidence to show: the agenda and minutes organized by sub-clause, the decision tracker, and the closure rate of the previous meeting’s decisions.
  • Question: where does the life-cycle perspective show up in design and purchasing? Why it is asked: the LCP now extends into scope-setting (4.3) and operational control (8.1) — one sentence inside the aspects evaluation is not enough. A good answer: point to the four checkpoints — environmental requirements at each design stage, in purchasing, in communication to providers, and the disclosure of transport/use/end-of-life information. Evidence to show: design-review records, environmental items in supplier-selection criteria, and sample communication records.
  • Question: where are the audit programme and objectives? Why it is asked: 9.2.2 a) demands an objective per audit — an audit without one is invalid. A good answer: produce the controlled programme and explain how the objective, criteria and scope of the latest audit were set, and how programme effectiveness is reviewed afterwards. Evidence to show: the controlled programme document, the objective statement and report of the latest audit, and the programme-effectiveness review record.

01Scenario-identification method (five steps, each with records)

  • Step 1 · Fixed sources: permits and regulations (incl. a trigger channel for new rules), incident and near-miss logs, site walkdowns (fixed frequency and route), community complaints and concerns, comparable industry events — five defined intakes, each with an entry point, an owner and a frequency, so scenarios never come from guesswork. Records to keep: the source register and every intake record — auditors ask “when did the scenario list last gain a line, and why”, and a blank answer means the intakes are not running.
  • Step 2 · Scenario library: generate scenarios per source, explicitly including low-probability/high-impact ones (major chemical release, extreme-weather shutdown, neighbouring-plant knock-on) — BSI paraphrases the basis as “all potential”, so the safe move is full tail coverage; auditors check the documented 6.1.2 linkage and tail coverage, not slogans. Records to keep: the library with unique IDs and traceable source fields.
  • Step 3 · Graded assessment: score each scenario by consequence × likelihood against documented criteria (the criteria themselves re-versioned with traces); the grade sets the plan depth — dedicated page / merged page / no plan but monitored. Records to keep: the criteria document plus a score per scenario. How auditors check: they pick a low-graded scenario and ask why it needs no plan — the answer must land on the documented criteria.
  • Step 4 · Plan mapping: every assessed scenario maps to plan sections, response equipment and named responders — no orphan scenarios between list and plan; response configuration follows 8.2 c) and stays commensurate with the scenario grade. Records to keep: the mapping table (scenario ID ↔ plan section ↔ equipment point ↔ responder).
  • Step 5 · Drill-and-review loop: run the annual drill plan, file the post-drill review (what worked / what failed / how the plan changes) and write revisions back into the scenario list and training materials; extend information and training per 8.2 f) to interested parties and contractors — the gap most often nonconformed. Records to keep: the drill record, the review record, the revision write-back, and contractor training sign-ins — four documents, one set.

03Annex A.8.2 official checklist (a)-j) to consider when building the emergency process, landed item by item)

  • a) The most appropriate response method — select it by chemical properties and release form (absorb / contain / neutralize / cover), write it into the plan and mark it at the equipment point; never leave the choice to improvisation at the scene.
  • b) Internal and external communication arrangements — internal: the duty chain and escalation path in writing; external: templates for regulators, the community and the media prepared in advance per 7.4.3 and your compliance obligations — nobody drafts wording on the day of the event.
  • c) Specific actions to prevent or mitigate adverse environmental effects — name the actions (containment, interception, storm-drain sealing) with equipment locations and owners; mitigation must map to the environmental effect, not a generic “handle it”.
  • d) Mitigating and responding actions for different types of emergencies — separate action pages for fire, release, explosion and extreme weather, with grading tied to the 8.2 c) proportionality.
  • e) Post-incident or post-drill evaluation — a three-question template: what worked / what failed / how the plan changes; the results written back into the plan and the scenario list.
  • f) Periodic testing of planned response actions where practicable — the annual drill plan covers every scenario type, and where testing is impracticable the reason is documented, never silently skipped.
  • g) Training for emergency personnel — trainees include persons working under your control and contractors (8.2 f)), with sign-ins and quizzes kept; content tracks the current plan version.
  • h) Lists of key personnel and assistance agencies — fire, spill-cleaning services, hospital toxicology contacts; verify them periodically (update on any number change) and keep the verification records.
  • i) Evacuation routes and muster points — site signage consistent with the plan maps, including versions for night-time and power-loss conditions.
  • j) Mutual-aid arrangements with neighbouring organizations — written agreements with one call-chain drill; informal neighbourly goodwill is not an arrangement.

04A full worked scenario (manufacturing: hazardous-chemical release in the warehouse)

  • Trigger: a drum of solvent drips during a forklift transfer in the warehouse; the walkdown officer confirms the release volume and chemical properties per step one of the plan — scenario L-03 is already registered in the 6.1.2 list, sourced from a site walkdown six months earlier.
  • Response grading: graded level 2 (in-house emergency team) per the documented criteria — level 1 stays on-scene, level 3 would trigger community notification and external services; the response force matches the release scale and its potential environmental impact (8.2 c)).
  • Notification chain: scene → EHS duty officer → plant manager; the external call is assessed against 7.4.3 and any notification duty under your compliance obligations; mitigation actions such as storm-drain sealing run per the matching plan clause, all recorded.
  • Recovery: released material collected and disposed as hazardous waste, contaminated soil sampled and tested, environmental-impact mitigation documented; the emergency state closes only after recovery is confirmed.
  • Post-event review: though a real incident, it still completes the 8.2 e) review — what worked (sealing equipment on site in five minutes), what failed (an expired night-shift contact list), how the plan changes (quarterly contact-list verification) — with revisions written back into the scenario list and training materials, every record numbered and traceable.

Red line: the 6.1.2 identification duty interacts with 6.1 “risks and opportunities” planning — emergency situations must be readable in both places (environmental aspects 6.1.2 and risks & opportunities 6.1.4); fixing only one still draws a finding. Update clause references in both the manual and the procedures.

  • Compliance register and evaluation — must contain: obligation↔legal-text mapping verbatim (clause number + source + version date), evidence for each “met / not met” grading, and 9.1.2 evaluation frequency with status-knowledge updates. Common gaps: obligation names without legal sources, or gradings without evidence. On audit day: justify two or three gradings on the spot, and be ready to show the legal-update intake (subscription or log); the compliance role maintains it, with legal re-checking regulatory changes quarterly.
  • External-provider control evidence — must contain: the relevance criteria for “relevant to the EMS’s intended outcomes”, the supplier list, evaluation and monitoring records for transport / waste / chemical providers, and records of communicating environmental requirements to providers (incl. contractors). Common gaps: control still stuck on the old “outsourced” list, contractors outside the communication scope. On audit day: present list + evaluation + communication as one set and pick one supplier to narrate what was evaluated and what changed; purchasing owns the list, EHS issues the evaluation conclusions.
  • Audit programme and effectiveness — must contain: the controlled programme (frequency / methods / responsibilities / planning requirements / reporting), objective-criteria-scope statements per audit, and programme-effectiveness reviews. Common gaps: the programme living as an email calendar, or an empty objective column. On audit day: open the programme original and the latest audit report cover — point to the objective line first, then the report conclusion; the lead auditor maintains it, signed off by the system lead.
  • Three-part management-review outputs — must contain: agenda and minutes organized by 9.3.1/9.3.2/9.3.3, inputs ticked off item by item (incl. changes in interested parties’ needs and expectations), and a decision tracker. Common gaps: missing inputs with no explanation, decisions without a tracking column. On audit day: show the ticked input list with decision numbers matching the tracker one to one, and quote the previous meeting’s closure rate on the spot; the management representative chairs it, with the system function sending the input call two weeks ahead.

A clause-indexed structure for the evidence pack: top level split by clause (4 context and environmental conditions / 5 leadership and policy / 6 planning (6.1.2-6.1.5 and 6.3) / 7 support / 8 operation (8.1 external providers and 8.2 emergency) / 9 performance / 10 improvement); under 8.2, four sub-folders — scenario library / plan / drills and reviews / training sign-ins; every file named with a clause prefix and date (e.g. 6.3-change-verification-2028Q3). A one-page index lists “clause → evidence file number → location → owner”, so any clause number quoted by the auditor lands on the right page within 30 seconds; keep the Annex A.8.2 a)-j) self-check page as the first sheet of the plan folder so the self-check conclusion is the first thing an auditor sees. Maintain electronic and paper packs in the same structure — for a remote audit, screen-sharing switches over at zero cost — and keep the binder order identical to the gap-analysis clause order, so an auditor jumping from the gap report to the evidence never re-learns the layout.
