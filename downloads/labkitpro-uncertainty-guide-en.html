Measurement Uncertainty,
A Practical Introduction

A plain-English walkthrough of the two internationally accepted ways to estimate uncertainty — the GUM analytical method and the Monte Carlo method — with real numbers, no calculus required. Built on ISO/IEC 17025:2017 and the GUM / Monte Carlo guidance (JCGM 100:2008 & 101:2008).

0The One Idea You Must Get First

A measurement result is never a single number. It is a best estimate plus a band of doubt — and that band is the measurement uncertainty.

0.1023the number you report
± 0.0007the doubt around it (expanded, k=2)
95%how sure you are it falls inside
Why it matters: your customer uses your result to accept or reject a product. If the doubt band is too big, a good product may be wrongly rejected; too small, and a bad product slips through. Uncertainty is the quality mark of a measurement — and ISO/IEC 17025:2017 §7.6 requires every calibration laboratory to report it, and testing laboratories to evaluate it whenever a numerical result is reported.

1Four Words That Cover 90% of the Topic

Standard uncertainty — u(x)

The doubt of one single source, expressed as a standard deviation. It is the building block of everything below.

Example: "this balance drifts by ±0.05 g" → u = 0.05/√3 ≈ 0.029 g.

Combined standard uncertainty — uc(y)

All the individual doubts combined into one number (square root of the sum of squares). One doubt for the whole result.

Example: uc = √(0.029² + 0.015² + …) ≈ 0.036 g.

Expanded uncertainty — U

The combined doubt multiplied by a coverage factor k so the band is wide enough to cover most possible values.

Example: U = k × uc = 2 × 0.036 ≈ 0.072 g (≈95%).

Coverage probability — p

The chance that the true value lies inside your band. 95% is the international default.

"We are about 95% confident the true value is between y−U and y+U."

2Method 1 — The GUM Analytical Method

GUM = Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement (JCGM 100:2008). It is the "bottom-up" method: take the measurement apart, find every source of doubt, turn each into a standard uncertainty, then add them up mathematically.

The 5 moves, in plain words

1 · Model itWrite the formula of your result y = f(x₁, x₂, …) including every correction.
2 · List sourcesCalibration, resolution, temperature, operator, sampling, drift…
3 · Score eachTurn every source into a standard uncertainty u(xᵢ) — Type A or Type B.
4 · CombineAdd them as squares, take the root: uc(y).
5 · WidenMultiply by k (usually 2) to get U and report "y ± U".

Step 3a · Type A evaluation — count the scatter

If you can repeat the measurement, statistics do the work. Measure the same thing n times; the scatter of the readings is the doubt.

Type A standard uncertainty of the mean:
u(x) = s / √n
where s = standard deviation of the n readings.
5repeat readings of the same sample
s = 0.22standard deviation of the readings
u = 0.0980.22 / √5 — your Type A doubt
Remember: with only a few readings the estimate is wobbly — that is what "degrees of freedom" (n−1) captures. More readings, smaller and more reliable u.

Step 3b · Type B evaluation — judge from documents

For sources you cannot repeat (calibration certificate, manufacturer spec, resolution), you read the information and convert it. Three common conversions:

Where the info comes fromHow to convertWorked example
Calibration certificate quotes U with kDivide by the same kU = 0.002 mm at k=2 → u = 0.002/2 = 0.001 mm
Manufacturer spec "± a" (no level stated)Rectangular (uniform) distribution±0.05 °C → u = 0.05/√3 ≈ 0.029 °C
Spec "± a", values near centre more likelyTriangular distribution±3 °C → u = 3/√6 ≈ 1.22 °C
Certificate without confidence levelRectangular, half-width /√3±0.1 % → u = 0.1/√3 ≈ 0.058 %
Key rule: do not double-count. If the repeat readings already include the scatter from, say, temperature, do not add temperature again as a separate Type B item.

Step 4 · Combine — square, add, root

The model result y is usually a sum or product of inputs. Two practical shortcuts:

Sum or difference (y = p + q + …):
uc(y) = √(u(p)² + u(q)² + …)
Product or quotient (y = p × q / r):
uc(y)/y = √( (u(p)/p)² + (u(q)/q)² + (u(r)/r)² )

Mixing a sum inside a product (e.g. (o+p)/(q+r))? Combine (o+p) first with rule 1, then use rule 2 with the interim result.

Where sensitivity coefficients live: when an input is in different units (temperature °C vs length mm), multiply its u by the sensitivity coefficient cᵢ = ∂f/∂xᵢ — i.e. "how much the result changes when this input changes by one unit". In the sum/product shortcuts above, the coefficients are already built in.

Step 5 · Expand and report

Expanded uncertainty:
U = k × uc(y), with k = 2 for about 95% (k = 2.58 ≈ 99%)
Report style (calibration certificate)Example
Result + expanded uncertaintyLength = 25.0034 mm ± 0.0012 mm
Mandatory explanatory note"The reported expanded uncertainty is stated as the combined standard uncertainty multiplied by the coverage factor k = 2, which for a normal distribution corresponds to a coverage probability of approximately 95 %."
Rounding ruleUncertainty: max two significant figures (0.0012, not 0.001234). Round the result to match.
When k ≠ 2: with few readings the normal assumption weakens. Use the t-distribution (Welch–Satterthwaite) to find the right k from the effective degrees of freedom — the exact method is in JCGM 100:2008 Annex G.

3Method 2 — The Monte Carlo Method (MCM)

Monte Carlo (JCGM 101:2008) is built on random sampling. Instead of propagating uncertainties with formulas, you simulate the whole measurement thousands of times and look at the spread of simulated results.

The idea in one picture

1 · Give each input a distributionEvery source gets a probability curve: normal, rectangular, triangular…
2 · Draw N random samplesFor each of (say) 1,000,000 rounds, pick one random value from each distribution.
3 · Compute the result each roundFeed the sampled values into your model y = f(…).
4 · Collect the histogramYou now have a million simulated results — their spread is your uncertainty.
5 · Read off the bandThe 2.5% and 97.5% points of the histogram are your 95% coverage interval.

Why the computer version is sometimes better

SituationWhy Monte Carlo wins
Highly non-linear modelThe GUM shortcut (first-order formula) can miss the real spread; simulation does not approximate.
Asymmetric or unusual distributionsRectangular, triangular, U-shaped — Monte Carlo handles any shape directly.
Inputs with very different magnitudesNo lengthy sensitivity-coefficient derivations; the model is evaluated as-is each round.
Correlated inputsYou can model correlations explicitly by sampling dependent values.
You want the full picture, not just a bandThe histogram shows the whole distribution, not just mean ± k·u.
Honest trade-off: Monte Carlo needs a model you can compute millions of times, and a computer (or spreadsheet) that does it. For simple, well-behaved measurements, GUM by hand is faster and perfectly acceptable. The two methods should agree within rounding when both are valid.

4Which Method Should You Use?

Use GUM (analytical) when…

  • Your model is simple and roughly linear (sums, products, standard formulas).
  • Inputs are not strongly correlated.
  • You need a quick, auditable paper trail for the assessor.
  • You are in a calibration laboratory reporting routine certificates — the classic case.

Use Monte Carlo when…

  • The model is non-linear, complex, or hard to differentiate.
  • Distributions are asymmetric, U-shaped or mixed.
  • Inputs correlate and you can model the joint behaviour.
  • You already compute the result in software — adding a simulation loop is easy.
Shortest answer: when in doubt, start with GUM — it is the universally accepted default and what most assessors expect. If the result feels forced (strange distribution, non-linear formula), switch to Monte Carlo. Both are scientifically valid; ISO/IEC 17025 does not prescribe which.

6Six Mistakes That Fail Audits

  • Double counting — adding a Type B source that the repeat readings already include.
  • Forgetting corrections — a systematic bias you know about but never correct, then claiming small uncertainty.
  • Blindly using k=2 — with very few readings the coverage is not 95%; use the t-distribution.
  • Too many digits — reporting 0.001234567 mm instead of 0.0012 mm. Two significant figures, max.
  • No explanatory note — reporting "y ± U" without saying k, distribution and coverage probability.
  • Ignoring sampling — if you sample and report for the whole batch, sampling error is a real uncertainty source.
