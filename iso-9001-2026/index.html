ISO 9001:2026 transition guide

ISO 9001:2026 transition: timeline, changes vs 2015, deadline

ISO 9001:2026 was published on 16 September 2026 — the first full revision of the quality management standard since 2015. This guide lays out the transition window, what changed, and how to get it done, with a ready-to-use document pack.

StatusPublished 2026-09-16
New-edition switchFrom 31 Mar 2028 only the new edition is issued
Transition cutoffTransition cutoff 30 Sep 2029

What this revision changes

The 2026 edition keeps the Annex SL high-level structure but adds a climate-change consideration to organizational context (4.1), strengthens leadership and governance expectations, and tightens supply-chain resilience and operational controls. The full clause-by-clause mapping is in the pack below.

Complete the transition in 5 steps

  1. 1Confirm your certification body’s transition plan
  2. 2Run a gap analysis against the 2026 edition
  3. 3Update documentation (manual, procedures, risk/opportunity register)
  4. 4Train staff, run an internal audit and management review
  5. 5Book the transition audit before the cutoff

01What actually changed (ISO-confirmed)

  • 5.1 Leadership: quality culture & ethics — Explicit quality culture and ethical behaviour; leaders must create an environment where everyone actively contributes to QMS effectiveness. On the ground: culture can no longer be a slogan — define 3-5 observable behaviours, 2-3 measurable indicators (first-pass yield, rework / cost of quality, awareness surveys) and keep evidence of day-to-day leadership engagement.
  • 5.2 Policy: aligned with strategy — The policy is more explicitly linked to context (4.1) and strategic direction — the QMS becomes a tool for business objectives, not a compliance ornament. On the ground: rewrite the policy intro, cite the strategy explicitly, and show the “policy → objectives → performance” chain at management review.
  • 6.1 Risk & opportunity: formally split — A clear separation between addressing risks (for informed decision-making) and pursuing opportunities (for continual improvement). On the ground: keep two registers — risks and opportunities — and tie every opportunity to an improvement project with an owner and a due date.
  • 7.3 Awareness: from knowing to understanding — New: people must understand quality culture and ethical behaviour, not just know the procedures. On the ground: upgrade training from process briefings to case-based culture modules, and keep sign-in sheets, quiz results and communication records.
  • 6.3 Change management: seven factors to weigh — 2026 clause 6.3 is explicit: before implementing a change, consider all seven factors — a) purpose and potential consequences; b) impact on QMS integrity; c) availability of resources and information; d) allocation/reallocation of responsibilities; e) communication of the changes; f) how effectiveness will be monitored and evaluated; g) how results will be reviewed. Most legacy procedures lack f)/g), the post-change evaluation — changes get archived, never re-checked. On the ground: write the seven factors into your change checklist, with records for f)/g).
  • 7.1.6 Organizational knowledge: retain, apply, share — Note: 7.1.6 Organizational knowledge already existed in the 2015 edition — the upgrade is in wording. 2026 shifts from “maintain and make available” to “maintain, apply and, as necessary, share”, so knowledge can no longer sit in a list; it must flow. On the ground: add a sharing mechanism on top of the knowledge list (mentoring, case libraries, rotation debriefs, offboarding handover templates) and keep records of the sharing activities.
  • Clause 8 review points: 8.2 sharpened, 8.3-8.5 carried over — After a line-by-line check of the standard text: 8.2.1 e) is sharpened from the 2015 “establishing specific requirements for contingency actions” to “providing information related to contingency actions, including any related to disruptions to the provision of products or services” — sales needs one more communication line for customer notification during disruptions. 8.3 design outputs (acceptance criteria and essential characteristics), 8.4 direct delivery by external providers, and 8.5 “availability and use” of documented information all read close to 2015 — treat them as review-and-confirm points, not new gaps to rebuild.
  • Internal audit: objectives for every audit — One of the hard new performance-evaluation requirements: each internal audit must define its objective — a schedule with dates and departments no longer suffices. On the ground: add a mandatory “audit objective” column to the plan (e.g. “verify the post-change review closed the loop”, “check the opportunity register works”).
  • 9.3 Management review: new input on interested parties — A new management-review input: changes in the needs and expectations of interested parties. On the ground: add the row to your review-input checklist, owned by the system function with an updated interested-party analysis as the supporting material — drift in customer / regulator / employee expectations now has a fixed entry point.
  • New Annex A (≈11 pages of structure clarification) — It replaces the old informative annex (Annex B is removed) with ≈11 pages clarifying concepts, terminology and clause intent, including clause-level notes A.4-A.10. On the ground: it is the best single reference for internal training — translate each clarification into your organization’s own language and keep the training evidence.
  • Climate change in the main text — The Amd1:2024 climate-change consideration is carried into the main text (4.1/4.2). On the ground: your context analysis must show a conclusion on climate — analyse the impact if relevant, and record the assessment either way; leaving it blank is a nonconformity.
  • Terminology & structure harmonized: 20 core terms in Clause 3 — The 2026 edition writes 20 core terms (3.1 organization through 3.20 monitoring) directly into Clause 3, with quality-specific terms (QMS, quality policy, quality objectives) entering the text for the first time; ISO 9000 remains normative — closer alignment with 14001/45001. On the ground: run a site-wide wording review of controlled documents — especially leadership, culture and risk vocabulary — to prepare for integrated management.
  • Documented information: “retain” → “available” — Documented-information requirements shift toward an “availability” basis. On the ground: review your documented-information list — relax what can move from “must retain” to “available on demand”, and strengthen access to what truly matters (culture indicators, opportunity-loop status).
  • Certification calendar (incl. accreditation milestones) — The official Global ACI-TECH-3-TR 2029-09-30 (M) timeline: published 2026-09-16; accreditation bodies (ABs) ready and announcing arrangements by 2027-03-31; certification bodies (CBs) submit transition declarations by 2027-06-30; AB transition decisions on CBs due 2027-09-30 — only then may a CB issue 2026-edition certificates; from 2028-03-31 new initial certificates are issued only to the 2026 edition; 2029-09-30 is the cutoff when 2015 certificates lapse. On the ground: before booking a transition audit, confirm your CB has passed its AB transition decision — otherwise what you get is not a 2026-edition certificate.

01Clause-by-clause reading

Clause 3Terms and definitions

Clause 3 now lists 20 core terms directly (3.1 organization through 3.20 monitoring); the quality-specific terms — QMS, quality policy, quality objectives — enter the main text for the first time, while ISO 9000 remains the normative reference. The substance: key concepts across your system now have text-level definitions, and controlled-document wording must line up with them. What auditors check: high-frequency terms in procedures and floor records (process, risk, documented information, nonconformity) against the clause 3 wording, and whether the manual still cites outdated definitions. The common trap: treating this as pure editing — the manual is updated but forms and record templates still carry 2015-era phrasing. The bridge from 2015: most terms carry over from ISO 9000, so a wording mapping plus targeted re-issues is enough — no structural rebuild needed.

Clause 4Context of the organization

The substance: determine internal and external issues relevant to purpose and strategic direction, and explicitly decide whether climate change is a relevant issue — the hardest requirement in clause 4, with 4.2 noting that interested parties may raise climate-related requirements. What auditors check: whether your context analysis carries a reasoned relevant/not-relevant call on climate, whether the interested-party register is refreshed annually, and whether expectation drift is tracked. The common trap: writing “not relevant” without an argument, or leaving it blank — both draw findings; others do the analysis once and never maintain it. The bridge from 2015: the 4.1/4.2 framework is unchanged — add the climate call and ongoing monitoring on top and keep the existing skeleton.

Clause 5Leadership

Among the twelve commitments of 5.1.1 a)-l), a new i) requires promoting quality culture and ethical behaviour, and k) lists risk-based and opportunity-based thinking side by side; a new NOTE 2 adds that culture and ethics show up in shared values, attitudes and established practices. The substance: culture is part of leadership accountability and must land in behaviours and indicators, not posters. What auditors check: observable leadership behaviour — quality topics in routine meetings, named owners for culture indicators, records of how ethics issues were handled. The common trap: running one culture training and archiving it, or defining indicators that never reach departments and that nobody owns. The bridge from 2015: the rest of 5.1, the 5.2 policy and the 5.3 roles framework carry over — add the culture/ethics sections and matching assignments.

Clause 6Planning

6.1 splits into 6.1.1 determining, 6.1.2 actions to address risks and 6.1.3 actions to address opportunities — risk actions proportionate to potential impact, opportunity actions suited to the context; 6.3 gives seven factors for planning changes, with communication, effectiveness monitoring/evaluation and results review now front-loaded; and 6.2 objectives remain an audit staple on measurability, monitoring and availability as documented information. The substance: opportunities must be systematically managed, no longer a footnote in the risk register. What auditors check: two separate registers, whether opportunities drive real improvement projects, and whether the latest change ran the seven factors with a post-evaluation record. The common trap: a register that spins idle — entries without closure; or post-evaluations that never feed back into the process. The bridge from 2015: 6.2 stands as-is and the 6.1 split is structural, so existing risk analyses migrate into the new frame.

Clause 7Support

7.1.6 organizational knowledge carries over from 2015 but upgrades to maintain, apply and, as necessary, share; 7.3 awareness adds understanding of the organizational quality culture and ethical behaviour; 7.5 stresses documented information being available where and when needed and suitable for use. The substance: knowledge must flow, and awareness must reach values, not just procedures. What auditors check: records of knowledge-sharing activities (mentoring, case libraries, offboarding handovers), whether floor staff can name concrete culture and ethics expectations, and whether up-to-date work documents are actually at hand. The common trap: a knowledge list refreshed yearly but never shared; awareness sessions handed out as slides with no quiz — attendance is not understanding; or superseded forms still printed on the floor. The bridge from 2015: the 7.1-7.5 subclause structure is unchanged, so upgrades are wording and content items — close them one by one.

Clause 8Operation (low impact)

The line-by-line conclusion: 8.2.1 e) is sharpened to providing information on contingency actions, including disruptions to the provision of products or services — sales needs one more notification line for disruption scenarios, the only real change in clause 8; 8.3 design outputs (acceptance criteria and essential characteristics), 8.4 direct delivery by external providers and 8.5 production control (incl. measures to prevent human error) all read close to 2015 — review-and-confirm points, not new requirements. What auditors check: customer-communication records in disruption scenarios, and floor evidence that 8.5 documented information is actually used — “available and used” existed in 2015, so the test is real usage. The common trap: rebuilding clause 8 processes as if they were new and burning budget; or ignoring the clause entirely and missing 8.2.1 e). The bridge from 2015: the 8.1-8.7 structure stands — verify references and wording item by item; this is the low-risk zone of your transition.

Clause 9Performance evaluation: two hard additions

Two hard additions must land: 9.2.2 a) requires audit objectives, criteria and scope defined for each audit — an annual schedule of dates and departments no longer suffices; 9.3.2 c) adds changes in the needs and expectations of interested parties as a management-review input, with g)/h) splitting the effectiveness of risk actions and opportunity actions into two separate inputs; and 9.1.3 evaluates planning, risk-action and opportunity-action effectiveness separately under the split wording. The substance: not new processes but better use of data. What auditors check: whether the latest audit states its objective and reports against it, whether the review-input list carries the new row with real content, and whether 9.1.3 conclusions follow the split structure. The common trap: objectives phrased as “routine audit”; or reviews run on the old checklist with the new row left hollow. The bridge from 2015: the 9.1/9.2/9.3 frame is unchanged — additions, not a rebuild.

Clause 10Improvement: renumbered

Clause 10 is renumbered: continual improvement moves up from old 10.3 to 10.1, nonconformity and corrective action follows as 10.2, and the old 10.1 general is deleted. 10.1 requires determining and acting on improvement opportunities based on analysis/evaluation results and management-review results — actions may include improving processes, addressing future needs, or correcting and reducing adverse effects. The substance: improvement needs real inputs, not only nonconformity tickets. What auditors check: clause references replaced site-wide, whether 9.1.3 and 9.3.3 outputs actually flow into 10.1, and whether corrective actions leave the 10.2 trail (cause review → action → effectiveness check → updating risks and opportunities as needed). The common trap: renumbering the manual only while procedures and forms still cite old numbers; or improvement stuck in reactive mode with no proactive evidence. The bridge from 2015: 10.2 content largely stands — the work is reference replacement and making improvement inputs explicit.

Annex AStructure, terminology and clause clarification (≈11 pages)

About 11 pages of structural, terminological and clause-level clarification (A.1-A.10) explaining the 2026 intent — A.6.1.1, for instance, states that risks and opportunities are distinct and can be determined and addressed through separate processes; the former Annex B content is folded into annex notes and the ISO/TC 176 website. For the first time ISO 9001 ships systematic supplementary guidance — a key benefit for new and existing users. The substance: Annex A adds no requirements, but it is the official reading of the standard. What auditors check: auditors themselves read the standard through Annex A, so training materials and audit checklists aligned with it cut communication cost and misjudgement. The common trap: shelving Annex A as reference material while internal auditors apply old-edition habits, drifting from the intended reading. The bridge from 2015: it is entirely new informative material — translate A.1-A.10 into your own language and keep the training evidence, and it has landed.

01Gap checklist (tick off as you close)

  • Give “quality culture” an operational definition: pick 3-5 observable behaviours (e.g. stop-the-line authority, nonconformity reporting rate) and 2-3 indicators (first-pass yield, rework / cost of quality, awareness survey), with measurement frequency and owners. Suggested lead: the management representative, with quality owning indicators and HR owning the behaviour list. Done when: the definition is in a controlled document, indicators are on a dashboard, and someone owns the data.
  • Rewrite the quality-policy intro: cite strategic direction and 4.1 context explicitly. Suggested lead: top management finalizes, the system function drafts. Done when: the new policy is published with briefing and sign-off records.
  • Split 6.1 into two registers: risks and opportunities managed separately. Suggested lead: the system function, with departments contributing entries. Done when: every opportunity links to an improvement project with owner and due date, and at least one has closed the loop.
  • Upgrade 7.3 awareness material: add case-based modules on quality culture and ethics. Suggested lead: HR develops the course; the management representative reviews the messaging. Done when: everyone is trained, with sign-ins, quizzes and communication records filed.
  • Add the climate analysis to 4.1/4.2: analyse impacts on interested parties and scope if relevant; record the assessment either way. Suggested lead: the system function or the ESG owner. Done when: the context document is updated and signed off.
  • Run internal training against Annex A: translate the ≈11 pages of clarifications (A.1-A.10) into your own language. Suggested lead: the system function; internal auditors learn first, then teach. Done when: training is complete with materials and sign-in evidence.
  • Site-wide terminology review: reconcile controlled documents against the 2026 terms (20 core terms in Clause 3). Suggested lead: document control, countersigned by process owners. Done when: a term-mapping table exists and affected documents are revised and re-issued.
  • Review the documented-information list on the “availability” basis. Suggested lead: document control, with using departments stating needs. Done when: the list is updated with recorded rationale for each relax/strengthen decision.
  • Consolidate change management: trigger, impact assessment, approval, verification, closure — evidence at every step. Suggested lead: the system function, with one pilot user each from production and R&D. Done when: the process is published and at least one real change in the last 6 months shows a full trail.
  • Land the 7.1.6 knowledge-flow mechanism: add mentoring / case-library / rotation-debrief / offboarding channels on top of the knowledge list. Suggested lead: HR builds the mechanism; senior engineers as first sharers. Done when: the knowledge procedure includes sharing clauses and at least one sharing activity in the last 6 months has records.
  • Add a mandatory “audit objective” column to the audit plan: one clear objective per audit (e.g. verify post-change closure, check the opportunity register). Suggested lead: the lead internal auditor maintains the plan. Done when: the plan is re-issued and the latest audit states its objective and reports against it.
  • Add “changes in interested parties’ needs and expectations” to the review-input checklist: the system function maintains the updated analysis. Suggested lead: the system function supplies the input; top management decides at the review. Done when: the checklist is re-issued and the latest review covers it with decisions.
  • Align internal audit and management review: re-cut checklists to the new clauses; add strategy alignment, culture and opportunity usage as standing management-review items. Suggested lead: the lead auditor re-cuts the checklist; the management representative sets the review agenda. Done when: the new checklist has run once and review outputs include decisions on all three new items.

How to run the gap analysis, in order (the order matters): Step 1, desk comparison — walk your manual and procedures against the clause-by-clause diff, marking each as met / partly met / not met; office work only, producing the draft gap list. Step 2, floor sampling — take the draft to the shop floor and check that what documents say matches what people do, focusing on change records, knowledge sharing and awareness training, the three places where paper is complete but practice idles. Step 3, interview verification — interview a few people at each level (top management on culture and policy, middle management on processes and registers, the floor on awareness and operations) to confirm real understanding. Only then set the ownership matrix and deadlines and sign off the gap-analysis report. Why the order cannot flip: interviews first bias you, and conclusions before floor sampling miss the gaps that never show on paper.

The six-step document-revision workflow (keep evidence at every step — on audit day this is your “revisions under control” trail): 1) change request — state the driver (which 2026 change) and the blast radius; document control logs the number; 2) drafting — revise per the points, mark the issue level and attach an old/new clause mapping; 3) review — cross-functional sign-off focused on interface consistency (a revised procedure forces its checklist and record forms to change too); 4) approval — sign per the authority matrix, verifying clause references are replaced site-wide first; 5) distribution — controlled issue and old-copy withdrawal completed the same day, void-stamped supersessions; 6) training sign-off — brief everyone within the deadline and file sign-ins and quiz records. Miss one step and the auditor will ask how this document was ever controlled. Sequence the twelve documents by blast radius: the manual and the five procedures first, templates and checklists next, the glossary last as the final sweep.

What the auditor will probe at the transition auditThe eight questions most likely on transition-audit day, with an answer-card method — for each, the form of evidence the auditor is really listening for.▸
TP-9001-08Applies to：ISO 9001:2026 Transition PackRev 2026.1 · prepared 2026-10

01What the auditor will probe at the transition audit

  • Probe: can leadership state what “quality culture” concretely means here and how it is measured? Why they ask: the new 5.1 turns on behavioural evidence, not paperwork — auditors want to confirm culture is not a values poster. A good answer: leadership quotes 3-5 observable behaviours and current values of 2-3 indicators, with who measures and how often. Evidence to show: the culture-indicator dashboard export and quality topics in leadership meeting minutes.
  • Probe: is there documented or minuted evidence the policy aligns with strategic direction? Why they ask: 5.2 requires the policy to reference context and strategy explicitly — alignment without evidence is not alignment. A good answer: you point on the spot to the strategy-referencing passage and trace how it cascades into quality objectives. Evidence to show: the new policy text, the policy→objectives→performance passage in review minutes, briefing sign-off sheets.
  • Probe: are risks and opportunities managed separately, and did opportunities actually drive improvement? Why they ask: after the 6.1 split opportunities must be systematically managed — “risks everywhere, opportunities nowhere” is the most common finding. A good answer: one full story from a register entry to a closed improvement — where it came from, who owned it, what was done, what it yielded. Evidence to show: the two separate registers and at least one closed case file with effectiveness data.
  • Probe: do employees understand quality culture and ethics, not just recite the policy? Why they ask: the new 7.3 e) requires understanding — read-aloud briefings do not count. A good answer: floor staff say in their own words what they do first when hitting a quality problem (report, stop the line, who decides) and how to escalate. Evidence to show: awareness sign-ins and quiz papers plus interview outcomes — the auditor will chat with people regardless, so be ready.
  • Probe: has one change in the last 6 months gone through the new flow? Show the full trail. Why they ask: the 6.3 seven factors are hard requirements — auditors verify the flow runs, not that it was published and filed. A good answer: narrate one change end to end — trigger, seven-factor assessment, approval, implementation, post-evaluation, and what the evaluation changed. Evidence to show: the change request with the seven-factor checklist, approval records, the post-evaluation form.
  • Probe: how was climate change handled in 4.1/4.2? Why they ask: 4.1 requires an explicit relevance call — “we never looked at it” is a nonconformity. A good answer: state the conclusion and reasoning — if relevant, the impact on delivery, cost and compliance plus the response; if not, still walk through the assessment. Evidence to show: the climate-assessment page in your context analysis (with the argument) and climate-related expectations in the interested-party register.
  • Probe: did Annex A’s clarifications land in training? Why they ask: auditors read the standard through Annex A — if internal auditors still apply old-edition habits to new clauses, your system reading drifts. A good answer: any internal auditor or team lead can explain what at least one A.1-A.10 concept means in your organization (e.g. why risks and opportunities are handled separately). Evidence to show: Annex A training materials, sign-in sheets, quiz papers and coverage statistics.
  • Probe: is ethical behaviour anchored in a mechanism? Why they ask: NOTE 2 ties ethics to established practices — auditors look for mechanisms and records, not slogans. A good answer: demonstrate one complete flow from report to resolution, including an anonymous path and anti-retaliation protection, ideally with a real (sanitized) case. Evidence to show: the channel description, handling records, and the protection arrangements for reporters.

02Answer-card preparation (one page per probe)

  • Write one answer card per probe: a one-line conclusion + evidence index (document/record numbers) + owner + data source — on audit day, landing on evidence within three sentences beats ten minutes of explaining.
  • Bind evidence by clause number into a “transition evidence pack” — anything findable within 30 seconds on audit day; the clause index page sits on top, and the electronic pack mirrors the same structure.
  • Rehearse one interviewee per level (top / middle / floor), consistent but not scripted — auditors spot rehearsals instantly.
  • For unfinished items, bring a remediation plan (milestones + owners); honesty beats concealment — transition audits allow plans, not hiding.
  • Walk the inspection route in advance: shop floor, records room, training room — 2-3 staged “show this” points per stop.
  • Carry the gap-analysis report — it is the first piece of evidence an auditor uses to judge your transition maturity.

03Official transition rules at a glance (Global ACI-TECH-3-TR 2029-09-30 (M))

  • The official transition-audit scope: the changed requirements of the 2026 edition (vs the previous), changes to your context, and any other needed system changes — organize your evidence under these three and nothing is missing.
  • The transition audit can ride on a surveillance or recertification visit or run standalone; it may be conducted remotely via ICT (IAF MD 4 / Global ACI TECH-3-003) if objectives are demonstrably met — remote is a legitimate option, not a compromise.
  • Extra audit time is “possible”: the CB must justify any addition per ISO/IEC 17021-1 and IAF MD 5 (TECH-3-004), considering at minimum the degree of system change and whether it rides the annual audit; a full system audit may need none — ask for the justification before accepting a surcharge.
  • Decline to transition and the certificate lapses at the end of the transition period (2029-09-30), possibly before the full certification cycle runs out — the CB may shorten the validity; the system keeps running but a lapsed certificate costs you tender and compliance eligibility.
  • If your certification body opts out: it must notify all affected clients in writing by 2027-06-30 (validity and consequences included) — treat such a notice as the trigger to switch bodies immediately, not in the final year.

Why the transition audit should ride a surveillance or recertification window: Global ACI-TECH-3-TR states the transition audit may be conducted with the organization’s surveillance or recertification audit, or as a separate special audit. Riding the annual visit wins three ways: fewer audit days — extra time is only “possible”, and the official note says a full system audit may need none, leaving the least justification room when combined; less preparation — the surveillance visit already assembles system-running evidence, and the transition checkpoints (changed requirements, context changes, other needed changes) fold into the same evidence pack; and scheduling leverage — slots tighten toward the end of the transition period, and a late booking risks landing after 2029-09-30, when the old certificate lapses with no fallback. Bottom line: confirm in writing, in your annual CB communication, which surveillance visit carries the transition.

Evidence-prep checklist for transition-audit dayEight evidence classes, each with what it must contain, the common gaps and how to present it on audit day, plus a clause-indexed pack structure — archived early, retrievable in 30 seconds, nothing hunted for live.▸

01Evidence-prep checklist for transition-audit day

  • Gap-analysis report — must contain: clause-by-clause 2015→2026 gap descriptions, ownership matrix, closure plan and a management sign-off page. Common gaps: no status refresh after issuance, or a sign-off page without top-management signature. On audit day: first layer of the pack — auditors judge your maturity from it first, so know your refresh cadence too; for open items, be able to turn straight to the remediation plan page.
  • Revision history of revised controlled documents — must contain: revision pages, approval records, issue/withdrawal logs, void stamps and an old/new clause mapping. Common gaps: old copies never withdrawn, missing void stamps, or superseded forms still circulating on the floor. On audit day: binder ordered by document number — any document sampled closes its history chain on the spot, with withdrawal logs showing names and dates.
  • Culture-indicator dashboard export — must contain: trend charts for first-pass yield, rework / cost of quality and awareness surveys, management commentary, plus indicator definitions and owning departments. Common gaps: undefined indicators, or data never discussed in any meeting. On audit day: show the commentary pages from the last two reviews to prove leadership actually reads and uses it, with a one-page definitions note at hand so you never improvise an indicator meaning — and a one-line explanation for any anomaly in the trend.
  • Opportunity-to-improvement case file — must contain: the full chain from a register entry to project initiation, verification and closure, with effectiveness data and baseline comparison. Common gaps: entries stuck “open” with no closure, empty effectiveness cells. On audit day: walk one closed case end to end, then show the full register to prove it is not a one-off — and be ready to say where the baseline numbers came from.
  • Training & awareness evidence — must contain: Annex A training materials, sign-in sheets, quiz papers, communication artifacts and per-department coverage statistics. Common gaps: coverage below 100%, or sign-ins without quizzes — attendance is not understanding. On audit day: archive by department so any named employee’s full record can be pulled on request; keep a catch-up plan for new hires and transfers too.
  • Policy-launch evidence — must contain: the new policy, briefing records, sign-off sheets and minutes excerpts showing strategic alignment. Common gaps: no all-staff briefing after the rewrite, or no policy→strategy passage findable in the minutes. On audit day: present policy text, sign-off sheets and minutes excerpts on one spread, mapped to 5.2 point by point; keep sign-off coverage consistent with the training-file numbers — never two conflicting figures.
  • Change-management case — must contain: at least one real change with all five steps documented (trigger → impact → approval → verification → closure), plus the seven-factor checklist and the post-evaluation form. Common gaps: a missing post-evaluation — changes archived once done, the most widespread legacy hole. On audit day: narrate the timeline page by page, letting evidence follow the story; if the post-evaluation changed the process or forms, show the before/after too.
  • Audit & review outputs — must contain: internal-audit reports against the new clauses (per-audit objectives stated), closed nonconformities, and management-review decisions covering the three new items with an action-tracking log. Common gaps: reports not structured around the objective, or decisions without a tracking column. On audit day: lay out the report cover and review decisions together, with decision numbers matching the tracker one to one — and quote the closure rate of the previous meeting’s decisions on the spot.

A clause-indexed structure for the evidence pack: top level split by clauses 4-10 and Annex A (4 context / 5 leadership / 6 planning / 7 support / 8 operation / 9 performance / 10 improvement / A Annex-A training); second level by evidence type (reports / records / training / minutes / dashboard exports); every file named with a clause prefix and date (e.g. 6.3-post-change-review-2028Q2). A one-page index lists “clause → evidence file number → location → owner”, so any clause number quoted by the auditor lands on the right page within 30 seconds. Maintain paper and electronic packs in parallel with the same structure — for a remote transition audit, screen-sharing the electronic pack switches over at zero cost. Keep the binder order identical to the gap-analysis clause order, so an auditor jumping from the gap report to the evidence never re-learns the layout.

Frequently asked questions

When was ISO 9001:2026 published?

ISO 9001:2026 was published on 16 September 2026 — the first full revision since ISO 9001:2015.

What is the ISO 9001:2026 transition deadline?

Certificates to the 2015 edition can be issued until 31 March 2028; after that only the 2026 edition is issued. Every certified organization must complete the transition by 30 September 2029.

What changed in ISO 9001:2026 vs 2015?

Key changes sit in organizational context (new climate-change consideration), leadership and governance, and supply-chain resilience; the clause structure remains Annex SL. The detailed clause-by-clause diff is in the pack on this page.

Do I need to recertify from scratch?

No. Transition happens within your existing certification cycle — typically at your next surveillance or recertification audit, as a transition audit. You do not start over.

How long does the ISO 9001:2026 transition take?

Most organizations need 3–9 months: gap analysis, updating the manual and procedures, training, an internal audit, a management review, then the transition audit. Starting early avoids the 2028–2029 rush.
